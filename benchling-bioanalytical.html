During drug development, companies run countless analytical tests to ensure the quality and efficacy of their products and processes. Developing them is a massive investment, where each test must be experimentally designed and validated. Novel biologics make this even harder, since cells are super sensitive and easily contaminated.

For companies like Merck, this process can be excruciating. It's like running a high-end test kitchen. Processes need to be meticulously orchestrated, with each step eventually documented and reported to internal auditors and government regulators. Scientists often complain about how hard it is to keep up with the sheer number of systems, physical materials, and data that needs to flow from start to finish.

Every handoff is a chance for something to slip

Analytical test development requires work that passes from person to person, system to system. Every one of those handoffs is a place where something can slip. A sample ID gets typed into a different system and comes out in a slightly different format. A spreadsheet gets exported, edited by hand, then reimported somewhere else. Context that made sense to the scientist who ran the test doesn't always make it into the report someone reads three weeks later.

Plate design is central to any bioanalytical software

A plate is a physical grid of tiny wells that slots into an automated lab instrument. Load it correctly and a robot can run hundreds of samples in a single pass. Designing a plate can be highly complex, with each well carefully mapped out to test a specific condition while accounting for evaporative effects or cross-contamination risks. Any system that handles plate design must be flexible, error-proof and efficient to use.

Where the handoffs break down

We led a design sprint on-site and remotely with their team for 2 months, mapping out their user journeys, digging into their systems, and understanding their pain points. We were impressed by the level of rigor that went into planning experiments, but also struck by how parts of their workflow really felt duct-taped together.

Disconnected tools, manual work

Different tools capture and document data, but they don't talk to each other. Scientists end up manually chasing down samples, checking expiration dates, and recording work they'd done hours earlier. On average, a scientist needed to work across nine systems to access the information they need.

Duplicative data structures

Different materials and instruments live in different systems, each with their own data structure. No one could agree on what a "sample ID" was, so they existed in four different formats, relying on know-how and Python scripts to get everything consistent. Multiply this across different systems and it led to a lot of frustration and confusion.

Processing data and reviewing findings

Robotics made execution faster, but also more data than teams could handle. Given all the manual steps in handling and processing data, the tedious work rapidly piled up. Lead scientists talked about manually reviewing reports, even on nights and weekends.

Seeing the end-to-end process

Teams didn't have visibility into each other's work, which led to confusion around timelines and requests. Every team wished the other teams understood their work better. When are my samples arriving? Where did the test results go?

The basic workflow

Underneath all the complexity, Bioanalytical had to support one basic path. A scientist defines a testing procedure: the rules for what's being measured and how. From that procedure, they design an experiment, setting the specific parameters for this run. Next comes designing the plates, mapping samples to the physical wells that go into the instrument. The plates run, generating data. That data gets analyzed, often through a template built for the test type. Finally, a lead scientist or quality team reviews the results and signs off.

Every decision in this case study traces back to one of those six steps. Some steps got the experience we originally wanted while others got a more pragmatic version, shaped by constraints we hit along the way.

Plates and structured data configuration are the two I'll walk through here, since they carry the throughline of the whole project, but they were part of a much larger set of decisions across all six steps.

Building two products on a single foundation

We made a major technical bet early on that we could build Bioanalytical on the same foundation as Bioprocess, another product under development. Both use cases required step-by-step procedures through systematic experimentation, and both needed those procedures to eventually be locked down and executed reliably.

Throughout discovery we incrementally validated this approach, but also identified gaps and risks. Bioanalytical required first-class support for designing plates that were highly integrated with lab robotics. We also hadn't yet built out a "locked down" version of Benchling, which was critical for automated sampling testing later in the project.

Exploring configuration under technical constraints

Configuration got the most of my attention, since it's the part scientists interact with every day. But building on Bioprocess's foundation limited what we could do about it in this timeline.

Structured data wasn't optional. Freeform notes and spreadsheets don't hold up when you need to trace a result back to a sample months later, and search, traceability, and analysis all depend on data living somewhere a database can reach.

So we explored structured entry that worked more like building a document in Notion than filling out the rigid tables already in the product, with scientists choosing what to capture and how to lay it out. Early concepts tested well on-site.

However, we eventually realized that we couldn't build this in time. The UI components, registration behavior and validation rules all made this basically impossible. It also required more of a platform-wide approach, as any improvements here should ultimately extend to our document framework. During our on-site design sprint, Bioprocess had already reached the same conclusion, leaning on Benchling's existing document table framework, making both our UIs stick closer to the existing components than we had envisioned.

We traded a better configuration experience for clean data flow that could ship in time, staying consistent with what Bioprocess was planning to build.

Rethinking plate design, still bounded by the platform

My first instinct was to treat plates the way I'd treat any other step in the experiment planning flow. A scientist works through experiment planning linearly, so why not add plate design as another step, maybe even auto-populate a layout based on what they'd already entered earlier? From a pure UX standpoint it made a lot of sense. Fill in the details up top, get a recommended plate design, assign it to a run, done.

Concept: Plate design during experimental planning

My version wouldn't work. Another team had already scoped plate design as a generic component elsewhere in the product, not built around Bioanalytical's flow, and wiring it into the middle of experiment planning would have taken integration work we didn't have time for.

I led a short internal design sprint on this, exploring options. The only viable path would be for scientists to just create the plate designs, one-by-one, in the documents after experiment planning.

We traced the impact through every layer, data flow to UI, catching rough edges like unclear parameter hand-off to plates, and how to make it easier to design a large number of plates across different documents once they were created.

The silver lining in banking this decision was that we also focused on improvements to the plate design UI itself. Because of the complexity of bioanalytical experiments, there was a real problem around visualizing the layers of experimental parameters and metadata within the plate. I did multiple rounds of hands-on exploration with the team, in particular pushing for a browser in the left panel, which I felt could be reused elsewhere in the product to tackle similar challenges.

Reorganizing around the work instead of our app teams

Looking back, hitting these constraints was never really a technical problem. We had pulled people from other teams into the on-site discovery, but their attention was split across their own workstreams, so our ideas didn't get scoped deeply until we'd already spent significant time fleshing them out ourselves.

Teams at Benchling were organized around app-level areas, but Bioanalytical needed work to move across several at once: plates, documents, data pipelines, the core experiment flow. We brought the resulting friction to product and engineering leadership as a structural issue, and re-organized into squads around a slice of the user journey instead, each with a lead from design, engineering and product.

In that new structure, I became a kind of connective tissue across squads. I set up weekly design reviews across all the teams, assembling designs and specs at increasing levels of fidelity into end-to-end flows.  These reviews also became a forum where all of us, including product and engineer, could stress-test how the pieces fit together, especially around handoffs.

{{ solutionLabel }}

The same six steps, now connected

Here's the flow as it actually shipped. Each step below carries the decisions we made along the way, some ambitious, some scoped down out of necessity, but all aimed at the same goal: getting a scientist from defining a test to trusting the results, without losing confidence at every hand-off in between.

Define testing procedures

Before, there wasn't one standard definition of a sample. It could exist in different formats across different systems, with no single source of truth, so scientists spent time reconciling versions instead of trusting the data. Now a testing procedure includes a standardized sample definition. It takes some setup, but once it exists, it means the same thing everywhere, and scientists can build experiments on it without re-establishing what a sample is each time.

Design experiments

This one was a pragmatic call. Scientists set parameters in a dense table, including a column for whether a piece of data belongs at the document level or the plate level. It gets the job done, but we knew going in that a table wasn't the ideal interface for a decision this significant. Better options were on our minds, like describing an experiment in plain language and letting the system populate the table, but they were future work, not something we could build in v1.

Design plates

Plate design didn't have a real home in the product before. Scientists now create plates individually within documents, with copy-paste between documents to cut down repetition. We managed to ship improvements to the plate design experience itself: metadata shows in a navigator panel instead of a dropdown, which makes it clear you're paging through layers of data. The same plate view works in multiple places, and data flows cleanly in and out of analysis.

Execute runs

Finally, scientists could see all of their work in one place, everything linked together correctly, and easily track progress, down to the step-by-step level within each document. We leaned on Bioprocess here, adapting their status tracking and step-by-step completion patterns for Bioanalytical rather than designing something new from scratch.

Analyze data

Datasets used to end up scattered in folders or embedded as CSVs in documents. Scientists exported to third-party tools or wrote custom scripts, both fragile and hard to trace when a result looked off. Now, data from a run is automatically linked to it. Scientists pull it into analysis with one click, run it through a template, and never leave the system. Nothing gets exported or reimported, so there are fewer errors and a clear line back to the source.

Review and approve

Lead scientists used to spend nights and weekends spot-checking spreadsheet formulas and clicking through notebook entries by hand. Now results are fully traceable: click on anything to see the sample, the notebook entry, the document behind it. If a result looks off, a scientist can check it in minutes instead of days.

One call I'd make differently is how we sequenced the two products. We treated Bioprocess as far enough along to start ramping sales and marketing, then turned to scoping Bioanalytical, only to find Bioprocess wasn't actually ready to sell. We pushed hard into Bioanalytical instead, which landed well, but then had to shift back to Bioprocess: an expensive context switch for the whole org, and months where sales and marketing were trained up on a product that wasn't there yet.

Of course, this wasn't a decision that could be solely made at my level, but I did learn that, as an organization, you have to be disciplined about setting a concrete bar for "ready to sell" before moving on to build elsewhere.

Keeping the team motivated through hard calls

This was a long, complicated project, made harder by a lot of organizational churn, including losing our head of design partway through. I got some tough feedback from our team early on when they felt like they didn't understand the approach and worried about shipping a product that met requirements, but didn't feel great to use.

Honestly, I felt it too, but I chose to focus on the bigger picture and what we could control. Going back to the core problem, scientists needed to us bring all these disparate systems into one place. As designers, we can pick apart a UI, but none of us had personally felt the pain of redoing weeks of lab work because an integration broke somewhere. We were building something huge and complicated that hadn't existed before and that kind of bet takes time to pay off.

Thankfully, it eventually did and, as scientists started using it, they saw the impact for themselves. 

The growing role of AI

Watching data entry eat so much of scientists' time reinforced something I'd underestimated: the highest-leverage problems aren't always the ones inside your project's scope. When Benchling spun up a small AI team, I made a point of staying involved from the sidelines, contributing use cases and feedback on where the pain was worst, because I'd seen it firsthand in Bioanalytical."
That led me into an adjacent piece of the work myself: integrating with external ontologies so the LLM could reference a standard set of definitions per customer, making its output more accurate. The lesson for me was less about AI specifically and more about staying attached to the org's next bet instead of considering my job done once a project ships.
