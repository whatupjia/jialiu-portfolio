Developing lab tests, running them at scale

During bioanalytical development, scientists develop lab tests that can detect and measure things. The level of precision and complexity goes way beyond what you might remember from biology lab. In many cases, these tests involve sensitive living organisms, complex lab robotics, and very expensive reagents.

Microtiter plates A plastic grid with wells that automated instruments can handle. Used heavily in bioanalytical work to increase efficiency and throughput.
Lab automation The use of robotics to improve accuracy and efficiency. Human operators are kept in the loop, loading samples, troubleshooting errors, or switching to manual workflows when necessary.

After tests are developed, they need to be executed reliably for weeks or months on end. The most crucial tests are for human clinical trials because you can't go back and get more if something goes wrong. At this point, teams are trying to smoothly run a conveyer belt of samples and tests, not doing real investigative work.

Complexity that erodes trust

The systems orchestrating the work are often patchworks of legacy software, custom tools and offline know-how. Scientists used up to nine systems to do their job. Each had slightly different sample ID format, materials were tracked in different ways, and notebook was so slow that scientists often waited until hours later to transcribe their work.

This often left the people in charge one asking:

"Can we trust this data?"
IND filing Formal filing to the FDA to test an experimental product in human clinical trials. It takes a huge amount of work to reach this point, so a delay or rejection is very, very bad.

They're right to be worried. Across the industry, around get delayed or rejected by the FDA due to incomplete data or poor documentation. It isn't really the fault of any one person so much the breakdown of an overall process and system. Customers like Merck want their tools to steer them away from that nightmare scenario as much as possible.

Spending weeks with these scientists taught me something that I kept re-iterating with our teams back home. It was easy to look at their Windows 95-era software and focus on how outdated they felt, but that wasn't the real problem.

The problem was that there were too many poorly-integrated systems, which led to scientifically useless work and weeks of waiting on data.

As designers, of course we needed to hold the bar on design quality and polish, but more importantly, we needed to design a solution where tasks and data flowed cleanly end-to-end.

Approach

Building on a shared foundation

Our approach was heavily shaped by an early technical decision before I'd jumped on the project.

For years, we'd been exploring a solution for bioprocess development, i.e. how to design and optimize the drug production process. We'd started working on a product called Benchling Bioprocess and the big bet was that we could build Bioanalytical on the same foundation.

On paper, this made a lot of sense. In both cases, scientists need to design and optimize processes through systematic experimentation, eventually locking them down for repeated execution. This gave us leverage, but also handed us constraints, which my PM and I were increasingly wary about.

Between Merck's requirements and the Bioprocess build-out, we needed to clarity as soon as possible about where these two products might diverge. We kept our design sprints with Merck conceptual, focusing on understanding their needs without over-promising a final solution.

This revealed our largest gaps:

1. How experiment planning and plate design interacted with each other

2. How samples, tests, and testing workflows would work

Experiment planning meets plate design

The good news was that our data model mostly worked. Scientists could define their procedure, plan experiments, and run them to get data. Importantly, we could join the data together automatically to make analysis and reporting much, much faster.

What broke down was how scientists could plan experiments involving plates.

Bioreactor Vessel used to grows cells and living material, which drug products are harvested from. Scientists start with small-scale experimentation, eventually scaling up to industrial quantities.

In Bioprocess, each condition runs in , which maps cleanly to each column in a planning table UI. However, a single plate can hold many conditions at once and their work could be split up across multiple runs. Basically, users could design and run them however they wanted, as long as it scientifically made sense.

The solution seemed obvious: let users design plates directly in the experiment planning flow. Scientists could fill out the planning table when helpful, but the plate designs would be right in the next step.

The problem was landing this all in time. Plate-related work had been passed around between teams and their original plan was to only make them available in our documents UI, where all our users could benefit from them. Different UIs built on different systems meant significant integration work.

The main alternative was splitting up the scientists work. They fill out the table in the modal (or frankly, skip it). Then, they create the experiment, navigate to a run's document and design plates there. Sure, the data flowed cleanly and this could be built in time, but the overall experience made me wince.

We went back and forth and explored every option, but we ultimately shipped this version. It allowed scientists full flexibility in how they designed plates and ran them, while keeping the door open for a more integrated experience. It was a trade-off I could live with.

Automated test ordering

Recall that the other problem was how to build out sample testing workflows. The workflow was complex but made sense once you walked through it, but the harder thing was getting everyone on board with it.

What is a sample? A test? Why do we need this?

These questions were more than philosophical.

Benchling's strength is its flexibility, especially for our current R&D customers. Plus, our team had been burned before by overfitting on individual customers, designing features that didn't generalize to others.

To help our teams understand, my PM and I walked through how our current system conflicted with the expectations of these new group of scientists.

Take the actual plate visualization, for example. Plates can have dense layers of metadata that help the scientist understand the design intent and double-check their work before executing. We'd previously hid this under a dropdown and I felt strongly that we should surface these more clearly and persistently. Beyond that, could we increase the size and visual density of plates, labeling values directly in the plate wells? It revealed an implicit design principle around plates that was being stress-tested by the complexity of the real work - scientists can handle complexity and they want it all there in front of them as long as it's useful. We don't need to simplify for the sake of keeping things light and airy as long as it's clear and useful.

Solution

The same six steps, now connected

Here's the flow as it actually shipped. Each step below carries the decisions we made along the way, some ambitious, some scoped down out of necessity, but all aimed at the same goal: getting a scientist from defining a test to trusting the results, without losing confidence at every hand-off in between.

Define testing procedures

Before, there wasn't one standard definition of a sample. It could exist in different formats across different systems, with no single source of truth, so scientists spent time reconciling versions instead of trusting the data. Now a testing procedure includes a standardized sample definition. It takes some setup, but once it exists, it means the same thing everywhere, and scientists can build experiments on it without re-establishing what a sample is each time.

Design experiments

This one was a pragmatic call. Scientists set parameters in a dense table, including a column for whether a piece of data belongs at the document level or the plate level. It gets the job done, but we knew going in that a table wasn't the ideal interface for a decision this significant. Better options were on our minds, like describing an experiment in plain language and letting the system populate the table, but they were future work, not something we could build in v1.

Design plates

Plate design didn't have a real home in the product before. Scientists now create plates individually within documents, with copy-paste between documents to cut down repetition. We managed to ship improvements to the plate design experience itself: metadata shows in a navigator panel instead of a dropdown, which makes it clear you're paging through layers of data. The same plate view works in multiple places, and data flows cleanly in and out of analysis.

Execute runs

Finally, scientists could see all of their work in one place, everything linked together correctly, and easily track progress, down to the step-by-step level within each document. We leaned on Bioprocess here, adapting their status tracking and step-by-step completion patterns for Bioanalytical rather than designing something new from scratch.

Analyze data

Datasets used to end up scattered in folders or embedded as CSVs in documents. Scientists exported to third-party tools or wrote custom scripts, both fragile and hard to trace when a result looked off. Now, data from a run is automatically linked to it. Scientists pull it into analysis with one click, run it through a template, and never leave the system. Nothing gets exported or reimported, so there are fewer errors and a clear line back to the source.

Review and approve

Lead scientists used to spend nights and weekends spot-checking spreadsheet formulas and clicking through notebook entries by hand. Now results are fully traceable: click on anything to see the sample, the notebook entry, the document behind it. If a result looks off, a scientist can check it in minutes instead of days.

Word got around inside Merck. Other teams started asking about Benchling on their own, and even as we shifted focus back to bioprocessing, we picked up two more prospects purely through word-of-mouth. One deal got far enough along to validate the whole approach and was on track to close within a few months.

Our dashboards showed more than just logins, too: scientists were running more experiments per user over time. We had no baseline to measure that against, but combined with what we were hearing directly, it added up to real adoption, Merck scientists actually working this into their day-to-day.

Leading through trade-offs

I love building things that are genuinely great, and I've learned to hold that ambition alongside hard calls about what's actually buildable right now.

Overall, it just created a lot of anxiety, last-minute meetings, and I think having some hard conversations up front could've at least prepared us better for the journey ahead.

Keeping the team motivated through hard calls

This project was long and complicated, and a lot of organizational churn made it harder still. Early on, I got some tough feedback from the team: they felt like they didn't understand the approach and worried we'd end up shipping something that checked the requirements boxes but didn't feel good to actually use.

I felt that worry too, if I'm honest, but I made a point of focusing on the bigger picture and what we could actually control. Go back to the core problem: scientists needed us to pull a pile of disparate systems into one place. Designers are good at picking apart a UI, but none of us had personally felt the pain of redoing weeks of lab work because some integration broke. We were building something big and complicated that hadn't existed before, and bets like that take time to pay off.

The growing role of AI

Watching data entry eat so much of scientists' time reinforced something I'd underestimated: the highest-leverage problems aren't always the ones inside your project's scope. When Benchling spun up a small AI team, I made a point of staying involved from the sidelines, contributing use cases and feedback on where the pain was worst, because I'd seen it firsthand in Bioanalytical.

That led me into an adjacent piece of the work myself: integrating with external ontologies so the LLM could reference a standard set of definitions per customer, making its output more accurate. The lesson for me was less about AI specifically and more about staying attached to the org's next bet instead of considering my job done once a project ships.
