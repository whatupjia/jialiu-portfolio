Benchling Bioanalytical

A unified platform to design and execute lab tests at scale

Benchling builds software for life science teams, everyone from university labs to the biggest pharma companies around. It started small and turned into a full platform that over 200,000 scientists and 1,300 companies now depend on, which is a lot of pressure for something that began as a handful of tools. In 2025 we launched Benchling Bioanalytical, built to help large enterprise customers design and run the tests that get a drug through development.

Background

Breaking the bottleneck

During drug development, companies run through a staggering number of analytical tests to prove a product actually works and can be manufactured reliably. Each one has to be painstakingly designed and validated. Throwing biological materials into this mix makes it even harder, since living cells are fussy and contaminate easily.

For a company like Merck, this reality can be grueling. Think of it like running a high-end test kitchen where every step gets optimized and documented, with auditors and regulators watching over your shoulder. Scientists we talked to kept coming back to the same complaints: too many systems, too many physical samples, too much data to keep straight from start to finish.
“The thing that was taking the longest wasn't doing the experiments in lab — it was getting data from all the systems collected and analyzed.” — Roy Helmy, Associate VP, Merck

Every handoff is a chance for something to slip

Analytical test development requires work that passes from person to person, system to system. Every handoff is a place where something can slip. A sample ID gets typed into a different system and comes out in a slightly different format. A spreadsheet gets exported, edited by hand, then reimported somewhere else. Context that made sense to the scientist who ran the test doesn't always make it into the report someone reads three weeks later.

Plate design is central to any bioanalytical software

A plate is basically a grid of tiny wells that slides into an automated lab instrument. Get the loading right and a robot can chew through hundreds of samples in one pass. Designing one is trickier than it sounds, since every single well has to be mapped to a specific condition while accounting for things like evaporation or cross-contamination. Whatever system handles that job needs to be flexible, hard to mess up, and fast to use.

The basic workflow

Underneath all the complexity, Bioanalytical had to support one basic path. A scientist defines a testing procedure: the rules for what's being measured and how. From that procedure, they design an experiment, setting the specific parameters for this run. Next comes designing the plates, mapping samples to the physical wells that go into the instrument. The plates run, generating data. That data gets analyzed, often through a template built for the test type. Finally, a lead scientist or quality team reviews the results and signs off.

Problem

Where the handoffs break down

We led a design sprint on-site and remotely with their team for 2 months, mapping out their user journeys, digging into their systems, and understanding their pain points. We were impressed by the level of rigor that went into planning experiments, but also struck by how parts of their workflow really felt duct-taped together.

Disconnected tools, manual work

Different tools capture and document data, but they don't talk to each other. Scientists end up manually chasing down samples, checking expiration dates, and recording work they'd done hours earlier. On average, a scientist needed to work across nine systems to access the information they need.

Duplicative data structures

Different materials and instruments live in different systems, each with their own data structure. No one could agree on what a "sample ID" was, so they existed in four different formats, relying on know-how and Python scripts to get everything consistent. Multiply this across different systems and it led to a lot of frustration and confusion.

Processing data and reviewing findings

Robotics made execution faster, but also more data than teams could handle. Given all the manual steps in handling and processing data, the tedious work rapidly piled up. Lead scientists talked about manually reviewing reports, even on nights and weekends.

Seeing the end-to-end process

Teams didn't have visibility into each other's work, which led to confusion around timelines and requests. Every team wished the other teams understood their work better. When are my samples arriving? Where did the test results go?

Data integrity kept people up at night

Bottom line, their scientists couldn't fully trust their own data, so they leaned on manual reviews just to double check everything looked right. When something did go wrong, it could take days or weeks to track down why. Worst case, they'd have to redo an entire set of experiments from scratch. That's exactly why reliable end-to-end data flow mattered so much to them, and why they came to us at Benchling for help.

Approach

Building two products on a single foundation

Early on, we made a big bet: build Bioanalytical on the same foundation as Bioprocess, another product we had in the works. Both products needed the same basic thing, step-by-step procedures built through systematic experimentation that could eventually get locked down and run reliably every time.

We spent discovery stress-testing that assumption, and it mostly held, but not without some obvious gaps showing up. Plates needed first-class support tightly integrated with lab robotics, which Bioanalytical specifically required. And we hadn't yet built a "locked down" version of Benchling, something that turned out to matter a lot for automated sampling testing down the line.

Exploring configuration under technical constraints

During the design sprint, configuring and editing documents initially got a lot of my attention, since it's the manual data entry that scientists often complain about. Structured data wasn't optional. Freeform notes and spreadsheets don't hold up when you need to trace a result back to a sample months later, and search, traceability, and analysis all depend on data living somewhere a database can reach.

So we explored structured entry that worked more like building a document in Notion than filling out the rigid tables already in the product, with scientists choosing what to capture and how to lay it out. Early concepts tested well on-site.

Meanwhile, the Bioprocess team had scoped a solution that looked much closer to our existing notebook configuration / editing UX. It was clunky, but familiar and doable. There's a lot of complex logic behind our document tables (registration, validation, audits) that make them hard to change or swap out.

Eventually, my PM and I decided to follow the Bioprocess approach. We all knew that our document config / editing UX needed work, but that needed to be a platform-wide fix with a clear path for maintaining and extending them.

So we traded a better configuration experience for clean data flow we could actually ship on time, staying consistent with what Bioprocess was already building toward.

Exploring experiment planning and plate design

My first instinct was to treat plates the way I'd treat any other step in the experiment planning flow. A scientist works through experiment planning linearly, so why not add plate design as another step, maybe even auto-populate a layout based on what they'd already entered earlier? From a pure UX standpoint it made a lot of sense. Fill in the details up top, get a recommended plate design, assign it to a run, done.

Concept: Plate design during experimental planning

The concept landed well with scientists and our sprint team, but ran into a snag during further scoping. It turned out that it'd require a lot more integration work than we anticipated. It was doable, but probably not in the time that we had. We circled up to work through the options and trade-offs: the only path that actually held up was letting scientists create plate designs one by one, in the documents, after experiment planning was done.

From there we traced the impact through every layer, from data flow up to UI, and caught the rough edges along the way: unclear parameter hand-off to plates, and how to make designing a large number of plates across different documents less painful once they existed.

The silver lining in banking that decision was that it freed us up to focus on the plate design UI itself. Bioanalytical experiments are complicated enough that visualizing the layers of experimental parameters and metadata inside a plate was a real problem on its own. I ran several rounds of hands-on exploration with the team, pushing hardest for a browser in the left panel, something I thought could get reused elsewhere in the product.

Reorganizing around the work instead of our app boundaries

These scoping conversations made us realize that we were moving too slowly given the work ahead. We'd loop in team leads for our design sprints, but it wasn't being distilled down to the individual engineers who owned detailed scoping, and their attention was split across existing work. Decisions were getting stuck in slow follow-ups and unclear ownership.

We raised this as a structural issue with leadership, eventually reorganizing into squads around a slice of the user journey, pulling people from different app teams together. Our entire team, from leadership to individual ICs, was finally focused entirely on Bioanalytical until it launched.

In that new structure, I became connective tissue across squads, running weekly design reviews that pulled designs and specs into end-to-end flows and gave everyone a place to stress-test the handoffs.

Solution

The same six steps, now connected

Here's the flow as it actually shipped. Each step below carries the decisions we made along the way, some ambitious, some scoped down out of necessity, but all aimed at the same goal: getting a scientist from defining a test to trusting the results, without losing confidence at every hand-off in between.

Define testing procedures

Before, there wasn't one standard definition of a sample. It could exist in different formats across different systems, with no single source of truth, so scientists spent time reconciling versions instead of trusting the data. Now a testing procedure includes a standardized sample definition. It takes some setup, but once it exists, it means the same thing everywhere, and scientists can build experiments on it without re-establishing what a sample is each time.

Design experiments

This one was a pragmatic call. Scientists set parameters in a dense table, including a column for whether a piece of data belongs at the document level or the plate level. It gets the job done, but we knew going in that a table wasn't the ideal interface for a decision this significant. Better options were on our minds, like describing an experiment in plain language and letting the system populate the table, but they were future work, not something we could build in v1.

Design plates

Plate design didn't have a real home in the product before. Scientists now create plates individually within documents, with copy-paste between documents to cut down repetition. We managed to ship improvements to the plate design experience itself: metadata shows in a navigator panel instead of a dropdown, which makes it clear you're paging through layers of data. The same plate view works in multiple places, and data flows cleanly in and out of analysis.

Execute runs

Finally, scientists could see all of their work in one place, everything linked together correctly, and easily track progress, down to the step-by-step level within each document. We leaned on Bioprocess here, adapting their status tracking and step-by-step completion patterns for Bioanalytical rather than designing something new from scratch.

Analyze data

Datasets used to end up scattered in folders or embedded as CSVs in documents. Scientists exported to third-party tools or wrote custom scripts, both fragile and hard to trace when a result looked off. Now, data from a run is automatically linked to it. Scientists pull it into analysis with one click, run it through a template, and never leave the system. Nothing gets exported or reimported, so there are fewer errors and a clear line back to the source.

Review and approve

Lead scientists used to spend nights and weekends spot-checking spreadsheet formulas and clicking through notebook entries by hand. Now results are fully traceable: click on anything to see the sample, the notebook entry, the document behind it. If a result looks off, a scientist can check it in minutes instead of days.

Reflections

Leading through trade-offs

If I could redo one call, it'd be realizing how the sequencing of Bioprocess and Bioanalytical created a pretty huge risk and pushing for scoping earlier. We treated Bioprocess as far enough along to start ramping up sales and marketing, then shifted to scoping Bioanalytical, only to discover Bioprocess wasn't actually ready to sell. So we pushed hard into Bioanalytical instead, which landed well, but then had to swing back to Bioprocess, an expensive context switch for the whole org, plus months where sales and marketing were trained up on a product that wasn't actually there yet.

Overall, it just created a lot of anxiety, last-minute meetings, and I think having some hard conversations up front could've at least prepared us better for the journey ahead.

Keeping the team motivated through hard calls

This project was long and complicated, and a lot of organizational churn made it harder still. Early on, I got some tough feedback from the team: they felt like they didn't understand the approach and worried we'd end up shipping something that checked the requirements boxes but didn't feel good to actually use.

I felt that worry too, if I'm honest, but I made a point of focusing on the bigger picture and what we could actually control. Go back to the core problem: scientists needed us to pull a pile of disparate systems into one place. Designers are good at picking apart a UI, but none of us had personally felt the pain of redoing weeks of lab work because some integration broke. We were building something big and complicated that hadn't existed before, and bets like that take time to pay off.

The growing role of AI

Watching data entry eat so much of scientists' time reinforced something I'd underestimated: the highest-leverage problems aren't always the ones inside your project's scope. When Benchling spun up a small AI team, I made a point of staying involved from the sidelines, contributing use cases and feedback on where the pain was worst, because I'd seen it firsthand in Bioanalytical."
That led me into an adjacent piece of the work myself: integrating with external ontologies so the LLM could reference a standard set of definitions per customer, making its output more accurate. The lesson for me was less about AI specifically and more about staying attached to the org's next bet instead of considering my job done once a project ships.
