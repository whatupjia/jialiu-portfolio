Benchling Bioanalytical

A unified platform to design and execute lab tests at scale

Developing lab tests, running them at scale

During drug development, scientists develop lab tests that can detect and measure things. They repeat the following process many times until they have everything completely understood, documented, and ready to execute.

After they're developed, these tests run over thousands of samples. The workflow shifts towards importing, testing, and re-testing samples with specific business logic (e.g. if result > x, re-test at dilution y).

Getting everyone in the room

We spent weeks on-site with Merck's scientists. I co-led discovery with a lead PM and eng, running user journey mapping and design sprints, bringing them back to our team to explore ideas, and then back to Merck to test it.

I saw my role as a translator between Merck and Benchling, translating their dense scientific language for our teams back home, and later, keeping a stretched-thin team bought into a project that hadn't been on their roadmap.

Complexity that erodes trust

The overarching problem we needed to solve was one of siloed systems that led to wasted work and expensive errors. Their existing systems were patchworks of legacy software, custom tools and offline know-how. Scientists used up to nine systems to do their job and their notebook was so slow that scientists often waited until hours later to transcribe their work.

It was tempting to look at their Windows 95-era software and conclude that Benchling would be vastly better, but the reality was that their systems were purpose-built for their business processes. Benchling often had the opposite problem where it's so flexible that configuration becomes a serious challenge, and the result isn't always the exact solution that a customer envisioned.

Designing with our data model in mind

Our thinking was heavily shaped by a technical decision to build Bioanalytical on a shared foundation with another new product, Benchling Bioprocess.

On paper, the idea made sense. In both cases, scientists need to design processes through experimentation and then execute them under tight control. Bioprocess focuses on producing materials, Bioanalytical focuses on testing them.

This gave us leverage, but also handed us constraints. As we ran discovery, we realized that the core data model held, but there were gaps and parts of the products which might diverge.

For the sake of brevity, I'll walk through two key questions that came up:

  1. 1.How do plate designs work within experiments that were originally designed for Bioprocess?
  2. 2.How can we manage testing workflows when our system isn't set up to handle its complex business logic?

Plates and experiments: a mismatched model

Plates are plastic blocks packed with tiny wells, letting scientists test dozens or hundreds of samples at once, whereas bioprocess relies on physical containers that each hold one batch.

This had consequences for experiment planning and execution: Bioprocess had built a planning table, where a single column represents an experimental condition (e.g. incubate at 10°C for 30 minutes).

Plates broke this model because they're much more flexible. You can load a single plate with a complex jigsaw of experimental conditions as long as it scientifically makes sense. Bioanalytical scientists might summarize a few high level details in a table, but they fundamentally plan, execute, and analyze in plates.

So the key question was:

How can we let scientists run experiments with plates while shipping something feasible given our technical strategy?

I partnered with my teammate who'd picked up plates-related work. A previous colleague had archived some concepts which we could pretty easily polish to a shipped feature, but they didn't address this thorny question because they'd been designed as a simpler notebook feature.

We couldn't simply swap out the table or even really rework it. The table was wired into everything downstream: how an experiment got created, how work gets executed, even how the raw analysis data is output. Forking the codebase and maintaining two separate versions was off the table. Plus, scientists do sometimes need the table.

My teammate and I explored adding plate design to a third step in the flow. It was well-received by scientists, but the integration work would be significant. If we invested there, it could turn into a development quagmire and burn time that we could invest elsewhere.

The one other alternative was what I called the "split path." The user could fill out the table, hop a few steps, and then design the plates in the experiment's worksheets. It was inelegant, but it functionally was like having a third step (just a couple steps later). The obsessive designer in me disliked it, but the pragmatic problem-solver in me agreed it was the only way forward.

The complexities of sample testing

Let's turn to another problem. For sample testing, it seemed like Benchling had all the building blocks to solve it. We had a robust, configurable schema system that includes samples and results. We'd recently launched a workflow engine that could route tasks between users.

So, when my PM and I pitched the idea of building a Test object, it received some pushback. The problem was, if we just stitched these features together, it'd deliver an honestly terrible experience for our customer and users. If we literally applied our existing data model:

Yikes. Workflows were already hard to configure and the UI was dense. But the real problem was the relational shape between samples, results, and tasks. We couldn't provide the status life cycle and business logic out of the box that customers would expect. And it's hard to sell something when you have to keep configuring it forever and still can't get the result you want.

Everyone understood the problem now, so my PM and I sketched out the general shape of a solution, with input from a lot of people. I pushed for a cross-object, hierarchical view of samples, tests and results, like in the above wireframe. It made sense and could extend to other use cases.

Alas, Benchling's flexibility again became a design constraint. Customers can define their own objects and relationships in arbitrary ways, so we can't know in advance what to keep in sync, and combined search across linked fields becomes expensive. Everyone agreed it was a slick UI, but it wasn't doable by our go-live date.

The feature we ultimately shipped largely left our search system unchanged, with the exception of some additional filters and a test-specific view.

Solution

What shipped

Let's see where plates and tests landed, keeping in mind that these are just two feature areas of many in the final release. Happy to walk through other parts, if you're curious.

Define testing procedures

Before, there wasn't one standard definition of a sample. It could exist in different formats across different systems, with no single source of truth, so scientists spent time reconciling versions instead of trusting the data. Now a testing procedure includes a standardized sample definition. It takes some setup, but once it exists, it means the same thing everywhere, and scientists can build experiments on it without re-establishing what a sample is each time.

Design experiments

This one was a pragmatic call. Scientists set parameters in a dense table, including a column for whether a piece of data belongs at the document level or the plate level. It gets the job done, but we knew going in that a table wasn't the ideal interface for a decision this significant. Better options were on our minds, like describing an experiment in plain language and letting the system populate the table, but they were future work, not something we could build in v1.

Design plates

Plate design didn't have a real home in the product before. Scientists now create plates individually within documents, with copy-paste between documents to cut down repetition. We managed to ship improvements to the plate design experience itself: metadata shows in a navigator panel instead of a dropdown, which makes it clear you're paging through layers of data. The same plate view works in multiple places, and data flows cleanly in and out of analysis.

Execute runs

Finally, scientists could see all of their work in one place, everything linked together correctly, and easily track progress, down to the step-by-step level within each document. We leaned on Bioprocess here, adapting their status tracking and step-by-step completion patterns for Bioanalytical rather than designing something new from scratch.

Analyze data

Datasets used to end up scattered in folders or embedded as CSVs in documents. Scientists exported to third-party tools or wrote custom scripts, both fragile and hard to trace when a result looked off. Now, data from a run is automatically linked to it. Scientists pull it into analysis with one click, run it through a template, and never leave the system. Nothing gets exported or reimported, so there are fewer errors and a clear line back to the source.

Review and approve

Lead scientists used to spend nights and weekends spot-checking spreadsheet formulas and clicking through notebook entries by hand. Now results are fully traceable: click on anything to see the sample, the notebook entry, the document behind it. If a result looks off, a scientist can check it in minutes instead of days.

Word got around inside Merck. Other teams started asking about Benchling on their own, and even as we shifted focus back to bioprocessing, we picked up two more prospects purely through word-of-mouth. One deal got far enough along to validate the whole approach and was on track to close within a few months.

Our dashboards showed more than just logins, too: scientists were running more experiments per user over time. We had no baseline to measure that against, but combined with what we were hearing directly, it added up to real adoption: Merck scientists actually working this into their day-to-day.

Leading through trade-offs

I love building things that are genuinely great, and I've learned to hold that ambition alongside hard calls about what's actually buildable right now.

The hard part was delivering an experience that actually fit how our scientists think and work. My PM and I had to walk through this step by step for everyone to see how challenging it'd be. Workflows would need custom logic. Performance would need upgrades. Most importantly, the result just didn't match how scientists thought about their work.

Overall, it just created a lot of anxiety, last-minute meetings, and I think having some hard conversations up front could've at least prepared us better for the journey ahead.

Keeping the team motivated through hard calls

This project was long and complicated, and a lot of organizational churn made it harder still. Early on, I got some tough feedback from the team: they felt like they didn't understand the approach and worried we'd end up shipping something that checked the requirements boxes but didn't feel good to actually use.

I felt that worry too, if I'm honest, but I made a point of focusing on the bigger picture and what we could actually control. Go back to the core problem: scientists needed us to pull a pile of disparate systems into one place. Designers are good at picking apart a UI, but none of us had personally felt the pain of redoing weeks of lab work because some integration broke. We were building something big and complicated that hadn't existed before, and bets like that take time to pay off.

The growing role of AI

Watching data entry eat so much of scientists' time reinforced something I'd underestimated: the highest-leverage problems aren't always the ones inside your project's scope. When Benchling spun up a small AI team, I made a point of staying involved from the sidelines, contributing use cases and feedback on where the pain was worst, because I'd seen it firsthand in Bioanalytical.

That led me into an adjacent piece of the work myself: integrating with external ontologies so the LLM could reference a standard set of definitions per customer, making its output more accurate. The lesson for me was less about AI specifically and more about staying attached to the org's next bet instead of considering my job done once a project ships.
