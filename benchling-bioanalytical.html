Benchling Bioanalytical

A unified platform to design and execute lab tests at scale

Bioanalytical labs often work across disconnected systems, turning work that should take minutes into days or even weeks. We partnered with Merck to build a solution to this, using Benchling's existing Bioprocess platform as the starting point.

I led design alongside a PM and engineer, evaluating where we could extend Bioprocess's existing capabilities versus where we'd need to build something new. This case study focuses on two feature areas that exemplified the latter:

  • A test object, which powered clinical sample testing
  • Physical plate layouts, which the original Bioprocess model wasn't designed to handle

We launched with Merck, our charter partner, in August 2025.

Developing assays, running them at scale

During drug development, scientists develop assays (lab tests) that can detect and measure things. They repeat the following process many times until they have everything understood, documented, and ready to execute.

After they're developed, these assays are run over thousands of samples. The workflow shifts towards importing, testing, and re-testing them using specific business logic (e.g. if result > x, re-test at dilution y).

Complexity that erodes trust

Like many large enterprises, Merck's systems were a patchwork of legacy software, custom tools and paper documentation. Scientists used up to nine systems to do their job, and their notebook was so slow that they often waited hours to transcribe their work. This amount of manual work and risk of error led the people in charge to often wonder, "Can we trust this data?"

It was tempting to look at their Windows 95-era software and assume that Benchling's platform would be an immediate improvement. However, their systems were purpose-built for their work and we had to do our diligence to ensure we could actually support it well. Our platform is known for being flexible and configurable, but that becomes a liability when customers need first-class support for highly-regulated work. It was a bridge that we'd begun crossing with Bioprocess and launching Bioanalytical meant we'd become a serious entrant in the development space.

Getting everyone in the room

I saw my role as a translator between Merck and Benchling, converting their dense scientific language to Benchling concepts that our team could understand. There was an overwhelming amount of information available to share, but we had to distill it down into insights that anyone in our team could understand.

We created user journeys and personas, but mostly, we constantly sketched out conceptual diagrams with accompanying data models, as that ultimately shaped what was possible in the UI.

Building on an existing platform

Our approach was heavily shaped by a prior technical decision to build Bioanalytical on a shared foundation with Benchling Bioprocess, which had launched some months prior.

On paper, the idea made sense. In both cases, scientists need to design processes through experimentation and then execute them under tight control. Bioprocess focuses on producing materials, Bioanalytical focuses on testing them.

This gave us leverage, but came with constraints and real risks. Rather than designing a purpose-built solution from scratch, we'd be trying as much as possible to use or extend existing concepts. As you'll see later, this came with trade-offs that we had to carefully work through.

Throughout discovery and design, our guiding questions centered around this tension:

Does our platform's conceptual model align with how scientists think and work?

Does this concept deserve to become a first-class object? Does reusing our existing systems break things downstream?

If we can't build the whole thing, what's the smallest piece that still solves the problem?

I'll focus on two key areas where this came up: plate designs and testing workflows.

A mismatched conceptual model

Bioanalytical work centers around plates, small plastic blocks with tiny wells, whereas bioprocess relies on physical containers called bioreactors.

96-well microtiter plate
Bioreactors in four sizes

Bioprocess had built a planning table, where a single column represents an experimental condition (e.g. incubate at 10°C for 30 minutes). Plates broke this model, with complex mappings between the column's parameters and a physical plate.

If we wanted to use Bioprocess's data model, we couldn't really rework or swap out the table. It was wired into everything downstream: experimentation creation, run execution, data analysis. It wasn't a fundamental incompatibility, just a mismatch in the UI that made planning difficult. That said, everyone, including myself, wanted to avoid forking the codebase and maintaining two versions, if at all possible.

Exploring plate-based experiment planning

I partnered with my teammate, who'd recently picked up where another designer left off. They'd left us with some prior concepts which were a solid start, but hadn't considered how plates could plug into a completely new product area. It'd been planned as a legacy notebook feature, simplifying some of the build, but also constraining what we might realistically be able to ship.

Given that scientists use both tables and plates during experiment planning, my teammate and I explored inserting plate design as a third step in the flow.

It was well-received, but the integration would be costly. An alternative was what I called the "split path." The user partially fills out the table, navigates a few steps, and then designs the plates in the experiment's worksheets. It was inelegant, but it still gave scientists the functionality and flexibility to run their experiments. Here, the need for instant, clean data overrode UI polish.

Stress-testing our workflows system

Once an assay is finalized, it needs to be run in a tightly controlled sample testing workflow. During a clinical study, the system needs to automatically assign tests to samples and help scientists find, test, and re-test them until the final results look acceptable.

At first glance, it seemed like we had the existing building blocks in place, but this fell apart on closer examination.

Our workflow engine was built to handle team-to-team requests, like requesting a sample from another lab. But test ordering needed things the workflow system didn't do: automatically assigning tasks to samples on receipt, tracking status through result evaluation, and encoding re-test conditions so a failed result could spawn a new task with the right parameters.

We circled around this problem for a while, scarred by a history of releasing features with unclear boundaries and overlapping use cases. Everyone wanted to avoid creating a duplicative, workflow-like thing for a single use case.

Proposing a new test object

My PM and I sketched out the shape of a proposed "test" object with a status lifecycle.

We tried to distill the solution to its most essential parts. Other legacy systems had status lifecycles for samples and results, but if we built only this, users could still find the samples they needed. More importantly, they could get results almost instantaneously, where their current system made them wait. When we compared the two options, we agreed that the test object approach was the right one.

Solution

Plate-based experiments

The final release covered a massive surface area, and in keeping with the rest of the case study, I'll just focus on the two areas discussed above.

For plates, remember that scientists need to define experimental conditions and then decide how they want to run them within individual plates. A detail I skipped before is that the assay procedure itself encodes definitions (materials, parameters, equipment) that can be reused across experiments, so we start here:

We added the concept of a "confirmation method," which tells our system what values should be passed down to a plate by default. These are default settings, so scientists can override them later during experiment planning.

After the experiment is created, scientists can insert plates wherever they'd like. At this stage they're still trying to figure out what the final assay procedure should be, so speed and flexibility are key.

Within the plate designer, the experiment's parameters are passed through. Scientists can use them here or add new values, if any come to mind.

At the end of the run, they can send all their data to analysis. Everything gets joined together cleanly and results are generated within moments.

Assigning tests to samples

Here we jump ahead to after the assay is finalized. Within the assay procedure, a system administrator defines tests and links them to the assay. This decoupling of tests and assays gives large enterprises more flexibility in modeling the measurements they'd take in real lab work.

Clinical study administrators specify which samples are expected and how they should be tested.

Samples are created with tests associated, with a status lifecycle and instructions on how they'll be re-tested. When results fall out of range, new tests are assigned and tracked alongside the original.

All work is captured as structured data which can be reviewed and audited, with complete traceability from start to finish.

Reflection

Leading through trade-offs

As a designer, I always want to build something truly great, but in the real world, there are always trade-offs. We left friction in the flow that, with more time, we might've polished away. But the pragmatist in me kept the goal in view: getting scientists end-to-end data in minutes instead of days.

I learned a lot about how to support a design team through a giant, complex project like this. Early on, I got some tough feedback: they felt like they didn't fully understand what was going on or why our approach made sense. Beneath that was a worry that, in our desire to reuse existing features, we'd ship something we weren't proud of.

I understood where they were coming from and didn't deny that it was a risk. By the time I joined, we'd already made timeline commitments to Merck and made a technical decision that we couldn't realistically revisit. I value honesty and thoughtfulness, especially when things get hard, so I set up more time with the team outside of the usual design crit to more openly air out questions, frustrations, bringing it back to leadership when necessary.

I think what ultimately helped the most was the time we spent with scientists, testing ideas and understanding what really mattered to them. That feedback loop did more to give people confidence than a thousand design reviews could have. Sometimes, what seemed mundane to us, like a giant table of test results, produced moments of genuine surprise and delight with our audience. It's a reminder that good design isn't always apparent on first glance, especially when you're designing for experts.
